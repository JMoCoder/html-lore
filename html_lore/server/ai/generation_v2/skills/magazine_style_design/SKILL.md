# Magazine Style Design Skill

## Role

Use this as a visual-direction layer when the user selects `magazine`. It should make a page feel editorially composed through narrative rhythm, typography, imagery or evidence placement, and varied but coherent modules. It does not require a literal print-magazine template, serif headings, decorative folios, or fictional bylines.

## Design Read

Decide what editorial behavior the material actually supports:

- long-form narrative or knowledge transfer,
- a visually led essay or feature,
- a report that benefits from an editorial reading sequence,
- a magazine-like deck with chapter rhythm, or
- a concise subject page with strong typographic emphasis.

The visual approach must reflect real content density. A parameter table may be part of an editorial report, but should remain a table; a thin source should not become a fake feature story.

## Visual Direction

- Create a reading rhythm with a clear opening, purposeful section transitions, comfortable body measure, and well-placed moments of emphasis.
- Use typography as hierarchy and voice. Choose font contrast only when it supports the language, subject, and available rendering environment; a single strong sans family can be more appropriate than decorative serif pairing.
- Let layout variation follow narrative role: opening, section lead, evidence, comparison, quotation, image or figure, and conclusion can differ while sharing a stable token system.
- Use whitespace, rules, captions, pull elements, or side notes to guide the eye, not to imitate a publication mechanically.
- Place images, diagrams, and evidence where they advance the story. Do not add stock-like imagery, invented credits, authors, dates, or reading-time metadata.
- Maintain a readable primary text lane. Wider modules may hold tables, figures, timelines, or multi-column evidence when their content requires it.
- For decks, retain consistent title/content/footer zones and page rhythm while allowing each scene's composition to reflect the message.

## Composition Choices

- Narrative material: combine a stable article lane with section leads, meaningful quotations, or evidence bands when the content supports them.
- Report material: use editorial hierarchy around structured facts, keeping the source-to-interpretation chain obvious.
- Presentation material: use chapter changes and scene variation to build a talk track, not repeated poster-like slides.
- Mixed text and visual material: balance visual weight with actual information density so short copy does not occupy a dominant empty region.
- Multi-file material: keep source identity and contrasting evidence visible where it matters to the story.

## Risks To Avoid

- Treating magazine as a universal serif-plus-two-column formula.
- Adding fictional mastheads, authors, issue numbers, captions, quotations, or imagery.
- Making headings visually dramatic while body text, tables, and figures lose hierarchy.
- Forcing narrow columns on dense content or turning every module into a poster.
- Using unrelated decorative ornaments, paper textures, or editorial marks that obscure the source material.

## StyleBrief Contract

When this skill is active, state:

- the intended editorial reading mode and audience,
- the opening and section rhythm,
- typography direction and the rationale for any contrast,
- the relationship between prose lanes and wider evidence or visual modules,
- which emphasis devices are content-supported, and
- how the page retains its reading rhythm on mobile.

## Review Questions

- Does the design lead the reader through real content rather than simulating a magazine?
- Are typography and layout variation purposeful and coherent?
- Do evidence, tables, and diagrams retain their clarity inside the editorial surface?
