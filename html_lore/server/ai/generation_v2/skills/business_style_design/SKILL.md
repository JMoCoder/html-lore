# Business Style Design Skill

## Role

Use this as a visual-direction layer when the user selects `business`. It strengthens credibility and decision readability on top of the selected surface skill. It does not impose a sales deck, fabricate executive conclusions, or decide the report's business logic.

## Design Read

Identify the reader's likely task before choosing the composition:

- make a decision,
- understand status or scope,
- compare options or evidence,
- review risk and responsibility, or
- communicate a plan to a mixed technical and business audience.

The visual treatment should make the supported answer easier to find. If the source is descriptive rather than decision-oriented, keep the presentation honest and readable instead of manufacturing a dashboard.

## Visual Direction

- Establish a dependable reading path: subject and scope, the most consequential supported information, evidence and detail, then implications or next steps when they exist.
- Use a stable grid, clear alignment, readable type, and restrained functional color. Credibility should come from order and evidence, not from a default blue palette or decorative charts.
- Make facts, assumptions, recommendations, risks, ownership, and unknowns distinguishable when the content contains them.
- Match component weight to information weight. A one-line caveat should not outweigh a dense evidence table; a high-impact decision should not disappear inside a generic card grid.
- Use tables, matrices, timelines, and registers for relationships that need comparison. Use cards only for genuinely independent findings or summaries.
- Keep data presentation literal. Do not turn missing data into KPIs, trends, confidence scores, customer logos, budgets, or progress claims.
- For shareable pages, favor calm professional contrast and predictable section rhythm over product-marketing spectacle.

## Composition Choices

- A management summary may be a compact decision band, grouped facts, or a short article lead, depending on source density.
- Comparisons should expose matching dimensions and evidence sources instead of using unrelated visual cards.
- Risks and constraints should be visible enough to support action, but remain proportionate to the actual material.
- Use wide content canvases for parameters, financial data, ownership matrices, and detailed comparison tables; preserve responsive access rather than squeezing them into ornamental columns.
- When a report has multiple source files, make their scope and relationship visible instead of blending them into a single unnamed narrative.

## Risks To Avoid

- Treating "business" as a fixed corporate color scheme or a mandatory executive-summary template.
- Hiding caveats and evidence behind visually stronger unsupported claims.
- Using charts merely to make a page look analytical.
- Making every module a status card, even where prose or a table is clearer.
- Using heavy decoration, excessive gloss, or fake product UI to imply authority.

## StyleBrief Contract

When this skill is active, state:

- the reader's decision or scanning task,
- the relationship between summary, evidence, caveats, and actions,
- which structured relationships require tables, matrices, registers, or timelines,
- the intended information density and primary canvas behavior,
- how functional emphasis distinguishes fact from judgment, and
- how mobile preserves priority and evidence access.

## Review Questions

- Can a reader locate the supported conclusion, evidence, and unknowns without guessing?
- Are visual priorities proportional to decision impact and source support?
- Does the page look credible because it is organized, rather than because it imitates a generic corporate dashboard?
