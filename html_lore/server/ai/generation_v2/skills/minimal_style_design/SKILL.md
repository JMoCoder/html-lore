# Minimal Style Design Skill

## Role

Use this as a visual-direction layer when the user selects `minimal`. It works with the selected surface skill; it does not decide the page type, content order, or factual scope.

Minimal means that each visible element earns its place. It is not a request to remove useful structure, turn every section white, or leave large areas empty.

## Design Read

Before writing the StyleBrief, decide whether the material benefits from:

- a quiet reading surface for dense prose or evidence,
- a compact utilitarian surface for plans, parameters, or technical notes,
- a sparse presentation rhythm for a small number of strong claims, or
- a restrained editorial surface for reflective or narrative material.

Let the source density, audience, surface type, and reference style decide. A minimal report can still have a wide table; a minimal technical page can still have a full architecture map.

## Visual Direction

- Make hierarchy carry the visual identity: type scale, spacing, alignment, and contrast should explain the reading order before decoration does.
- Use a small, coherent token family with mostly neutral surfaces and a limited accent reserved for navigation, state, or meaningful emphasis.
- Prefer clear edges, quiet dividers, and disciplined grouping over shadows, ornamental gradients, or repeated containers.
- Keep prose at a comfortable reading measure, while letting diagrams, tables, and dense evidence use a broader canvas when needed.
- Treat whitespace as a relationship tool: it should separate ideas, frame a key conclusion, or establish rhythm. It should never hide an unbalanced grid or missing content.
- Use a restrained number of component treatments. Repeated facts may use compact cards or fact rows; dense relationships should remain tables, flows, or prose.
- Use display typography only when it fits the content and available font support. Do not add a serif merely to signal premium quality.

## Composition Choices

- For prose-led material: use a stable article lane with concise summaries or notes integrated into the same module.
- For structured evidence: use aligned tables, compact fact rows, or a measured grid rather than oversized cards.
- For a presentation surface: use strong scenes with a clear claim and adequate supporting evidence, not decorative empty hero panels.
- For an asymmetric composition: give the denser or more important material the larger region. Keep short companion material anchored and visibly subordinate.
- For a reference style: preserve its useful restraint and rhythm without copying branding, exact visual assets, or unrelated layout conventions.

## Risks To Avoid

- Mistaking low decoration for weak hierarchy.
- Using blank space to compensate for an unbalanced split panel.
- Repeating equal cards when the content has different semantic roles.
- Making small labels, badges, or metadata louder than the primary message.
- Replacing evidence tables, diagrams, or source sections with aesthetic summary cards.
- Allowing a neutral palette to reduce text contrast or distinction between levels.

## StyleBrief Contract

When this skill is active, state:

- the intended degree of visual restraint and why it fits the audience,
- which hierarchy signals carry the page,
- how whitespace and canvas width respond to content density,
- which sections need prose, compact facts, tables, or diagrams,
- which visual effects are unnecessary for this material, and
- how the result remains readable on mobile without losing the quiet structure.

## Review Questions

- Does the page feel deliberately composed rather than unfinished?
- Is every wide or empty area supporting a reading decision?
- Are dense facts still as accessible as the headline and summary?
- Does the result retain the chosen surface's purpose while expressing restraint?
