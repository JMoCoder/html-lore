# Retro Style Design Skill

## Role

Use this as a visual-direction layer when the user selects `retro`. It should establish an intentional period-inspired design language while preserving the selected surface's usability and source fidelity. It is not permission to add nostalgia-themed decoration indiscriminately.

## Design Read

Infer the appropriate retro register from the subject, audience, reference material, and medium:

- print or editorial heritage,
- archival, institutional, or technical-document character,
- playful analogue or cultural nostalgia,
- early-digital or terminal-influenced visual language, or
- a restrained contemporary page with selected historical cues.

Use one coherent register. Do not blend unrelated eras or add nostalgia where the material needs sober business or technical clarity.

## Visual Direction

- Establish the period through a coordinated choice of typography, palette temperature, borders, texture restraint, labels, and spacing rhythm.
- Let the reading hierarchy remain contemporary and clear even when the visual language references print, analogue media, or early digital systems.
- Favor a limited palette with deliberate contrast. Use color as a system, not a collection of random vintage tones.
- Use typographic character only where it supports the subject and language. Ensure CJK and Latin text both remain readable and do not rely on unavailable novelty fonts.
- Use textures, rules, grain, grids, stamps, or patterned details as subordinate framing. They must not pass through text or obscure tables and diagrams.
- Match representation to content: historical timelines, archival notes, labelled figures, and editorial callouts may fit; matrices, parameters, and risks still need clear structured treatments.
- A reference style may guide period cues, but do not copy proprietary marks, logos, photos, or exact branded layouts.

## Composition Choices

- For long-form reading: use editorial rhythm, measured columns, section rules, captions, and occasional pull elements only when supported by content.
- For a presentation: keep title, content, and footer/page zones consistent while allowing period cues in the canvas, type, and transitions between scenes.
- For a technical or business artifact: apply retro character to the visual system, not to the truthfulness or precision of data presentation.
- For sparse material: use one or two strong period signals rather than filling whitespace with themed ornaments.

## Risks To Avoid

- Mixing multiple eras, unrelated patterns, or novelty fonts until the result becomes costume-like.
- Sacrificing contrast, responsive behavior, or table readability for texture.
- Using fake stamps, dates, labels, statistics, or archival provenance that the source does not provide.
- Turning every panel into a poster or using decorative noise as a substitute for hierarchy.
- Making a retro preference override a supplied reference style or a surface's functional needs.

## StyleBrief Contract

When this skill is active, state:

- the inferred retro register and why it fits the material,
- the small set of visual signals that carry it,
- the expected hierarchy and readability safeguards,
- which content modules may use editorial or archival treatment,
- how texture, pattern, or period detail remains subordinate, and
- how the style adapts for mobile and dense evidence.

## Review Questions

- Does the page feel like one coherent visual language rather than a collection of retro decorations?
- Can every source fact, table, and diagram still be read quickly?
- Would the artifact remain useful if the period cues were muted?
